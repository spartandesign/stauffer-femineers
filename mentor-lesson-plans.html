Glow Up Your Badge — 20-Minute Workshop

Students personalize and assemble a tested no-solder light-up badge while learning that an LED needs energy and a complete circuit path.

Stauffer Middle School station · 20 minutes per district rotation · Theme-neutral

Canvas Reflection0 points · photo + three short answers · completed later
Prototype gate — not event-ready yet: The existing LED-strip shopping-list image is only a concept option. Do not release final materials, wiring/orientation directions, kit quantities, or student placement photos until one real badge design passes the 10–12 minute novice build, safety inspection, repeated-use reset, and 90–95% normal-success targets.

Objective

Students will create a functioning wearable badge and demonstrate that a complete electrical circuit is required to light an LED.

Success criteria

  • Student has a completed badge.
  • The LED lights.
  • Student can identify the battery and LED.
  • Student can explain that the circuit must be complete.
  • Battery, conductive connection, and wearable attachment pass the final mentor check.

Final kit structure

After prototype approval, prepare one complete, pre-sorted kit per participant: badge base/insert, tested LED or light module, compatible battery/power module, approved no-solder conductive connection, secure attachment hardware, and fast decoration components.

Spare electronics: prepare an additional 10–15% known-good batteries, LEDs/light modules, and conductive connectors, calculated from confirmed rotation headcount.

Do not substitute: Stauffer’s ordered addressable WS2812B strips and project electronics are not the generic two-wire LED-strip option shown in the concept image.

Before the event

  • Choose one final electrical design and resolve the exact LED, power source, conductive connection, badge base, attachment, and mentor-preassembled portions.
  • Build and test the final prototype; time it with actual novice students when possible.
  • Simplify any step that pushes construction beyond 10–12 minutes.
  • Build 3–5 additional prototypes and test repeated reliability, wear, removal, and reset.
  • Confirm district headcount; prepare one kit per seat plus 10–15% spare consumable electronics.
  • Test every battery and LED/light module before bagging.
  • Prepare one simple finished badge, one highly personalized badge, one alternate visual design, and one exposed/open demonstration circuit.
  • Prepare a known-good troubleshooting kit and a separate damaged/inspect bin.
  • Print or display the final orientation and placement card; practice the two-minute explanation.

Seat and table setup

  • Place one complete individual kit at every student seat or stage the same parts for zero-wait distribution.
  • Keep shared markers, stickers, foam shapes, and other tested decorations centrally reachable.
  • Keep small electronics in trays rather than loose on the table.
  • Keep mentor-only spares and troubleshooting tools separate from student materials.
  • Place scrap, return, inspect, and ready-for-next-group bins where students can see them.
  • Display one large finished example, the open circuit, the seven build steps, and the countdown.

Two-minute demonstration script

  1. Show the finish: “You are making a badge that combines your design with one simple light circuit.”
  2. Name the parts: “The battery provides energy. The LED produces light.”
  3. Show the path: “The LED lights only when the approved path is complete.”
  4. Show direction only if needed: point to the final kit’s +/− or orientation marks.
  5. Test before closing: demonstrate one open connection, one complete connection, and the safe stop condition.

Do not add a long electronics lecture or connect the activity to Stauffer’s Limitless theme.

Mentor roles

  • Lead mentor: welcome, demonstrate, keep time.
  • Build mentors: support assembly, check orientation, help students test.
  • Troubleshooting mentor: handle swaps, known-good tests, and difficult repairs.
  • Reset mentor, if staffing allows: restock kits, collect scraps, return tools, reset seats.

Adults and high-school mentors may overlap roles, but one person must own time and one must own the spare/inspect system.

Rapid troubleshooting

  1. Check the battery seating, connection, and switch if present.
  2. Check LED polarity/orientation if the final design requires it.
  3. Check circuit continuity and approved contact points.
  4. Replace the battery with a known-good battery.
  5. Replace the LED/light module with a known-good one.
  6. Repair the connection only if a quick swap does not solve it.
Do not spend several minutes debugging one badge. Swap a questionable component and move the original to the inspect bin.

Safety + reset checklist

  • No student soldering and no improvised wiring.
  • No exposed sharp component or unsecured conductive tail.
  • Replace damaged, leaking, warm, or questionable batteries immediately.
  • Secure the battery and wearable attachment before a student puts on the badge.
  • Use only prototype-approved tools and adhesives.
  • Return shared tools and all loose electronics.
  • Discard scraps; restock one complete kit at every seat.
  • Count known-good spares and move failed parts to the inspect bin.

Workshop readiness decision

  • Typical novice construction is 10–12 minutes or less.
  • Most students finish comfortably within 20 minutes.
  • At least 90–95% of badges work with normal quick troubleshooting.
  • Reset between groups is fast and repeatable.
  • Mentors are coaching rather than doing most of the construction.
  • Students can leave wearing a secure, functioning badge.

If any target fails: simplify the circuit, reduce assembly choices, increase preassembly, improve orientation markings, or change the component before publishing final student directions.
